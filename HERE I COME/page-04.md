# PAGE 4: THE FIRST LESSON

---

The first lesson HERB taught me was this: you cannot want something you're ashamed of.

We were sitting in his kitchen in Collingwood, two in the morning, both of us high as satellites, and he was telling me about a man he'd been with the week before — a married accountant from Geelong who'd driven two hours just to get on his knees in HERB's kitchen, a man who spent his whole life in pressed shirts and sensible shoes and perfect presentations, a man who had never told anyone what he wanted until he told HERB. The man had cried afterward. Not because he was sad. Because he was relieved. Because for fifty-seven minutes he had been exactly who he was, and no one had judged him for it, and he didn't know what to do with that kind of freedom after forty-three years of never having it.

And HERB said to me, "You know what his problem was? Not the marriage. Not the closet. His problem was that he was ashamed of wanting. He'd been shamed so long — by his father, by his church, by the culture, by himself — that even when he finally got what he wanted, he couldn't enjoy it. He kept apologizing. He kept saying 'sorry' while I was sucking him off. Sorry. Sorry. Sorry. Like he was taking something from me instead of both of us receiving something beautiful."

HERB pulled the pipe out of his mouth and looked at me with those eyes that saw everything — not just what I was saying but what I wasn't saying, what I was hiding, what I had buried so deep I didn't even know it was there anymore. And he said, "If you're going to do this — if you're going to put your mouth on men and your hands on men and your whole self into men — you have to own it. Completely. You have to want it without apology. You have to believe, in your deepest self, that what you want is good. Not just acceptable. Not just tolerated. GOOD."

I thought about that for a long time.

I thought about Danny in the corner, wanting, unable to move. I thought about every man I'd seen since then who was frozen in the same way — who wanted and wanted and wanted and couldn't take the step because there was a voice in their head telling them they were wrong for wanting, that this was dirty, that this was shameful, that this was something to be hidden and apologized for and eventually — maybe — if they were lucky — gotten over.

The voice was lying.

The voice is always lying.

The first lesson is: you cannot want something you're ashamed of. Shame is the wall. Shame is the lock. Shame is the reason you haven't done this yet. Shame is why you're reading this book in secret, late at night, with the door locked and your hand not quite where you want it to be yet. Shame is the reason you've spent years telling yourself that you can live without this, that the wanting will fade, that you can build a life around the hole in your chest and pretend it's not there.

It is there.

It will always be there.

And you cannot fill it with anything else. You cannot fill it with work or money or achievement or the approval of people who don't know what you're hiding. You cannot fill it with women if you're a man who wants men. You cannot fill it with silence. You can only fill it with the truth.

So the first thing you have to do is look at what you want and say: This is good. I am allowed to want this. I am allowed to have this. I am going to take this.

Not someday. Not eventually. Now.

And then you have to go get it.

That's it. That's the first lesson. Everything else in this book is just details.
