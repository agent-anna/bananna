## A NOTE BEFORE WE BEGIN

This is not a book about a bathroom.

This is a book about a boy who went to a bathroom every day for one hundred days because he was too afraid to go anywhere else. He took a spiral notebook with a Bic pen and a hunger that had no name. He watched. He listened. He learned.

I was that boy.

I was sixteen in the autumn of 1996. I had hair I didn't know what to do with, a Walkman full of tapes I'd recorded off the radio, and a suspicion that the world was happening somewhere else. The bathroom was the only place where I wasn't invisible. Because everyone, at least once a day, has to take off the mask and stand over a bowl of porcelain with their pants around their ankles.

This is a book about what I saw there. It is a book about the hundred old men who became my companions, my teachers, my mirrors. It is a book about learning to love people before you understand them.

Some of this will make you laugh. Some of it will make you uncomfortable. All of it is true.

And yes, I was a slut. Not the kind that gets a bad name. The other kind. The kind that opens himself wide open to the world and says: *show me everything. I want to learn how to feel.*

That's the kind of slut I became.

This is how.

## DAY 0 — THE DECISION

The first time I went to the men's room at the Central Terminal, I didn't go in. I stood outside for ten minutes pretending to check the bus schedule. I was a boy with a new notebook and no idea how to be a writer. But I knew writers went places where people were honest. And the men's room at a bus station at 5:47 in the afternoon seemed like the most honest place I could afford.

So I made a rule: one hundred days. One visit per day. No skipping. I would write down whatever I saw. I would not look away.

I was sixteen. I thought I was looking for material.

I was actually looking for myself.

# PART ONE: THE WATCHER
## Days 1–20

**Day 1 — The First Fear**
I went in. The fluorescent lights hummed. The tiles were the color of butterscotch and regret. I stood at the sink for a long time pretending to wash my hands. I learned that a man can wash his hands for exactly forty-three seconds before it becomes weird. Then I sat in the corner stall, opened my notebook, and wrote: *I am here.* By the end of the day I had used two sheets of toilet paper pretending to clean my nose. Nobody noticed. That was the first lesson: nobody is looking at you. They're all looking in the mirror.

**Day 2 — The Sigh**
A businessman in a gray suit walked to the urinal, unzipped, and let out a sigh so deep it sounded like the last breath of a dying tire. Something about that sigh was older than him. I wrote: *Everybody holds stress in their bladder.*

**Day 3 — The Pee-Shy Boy**
I tried to use the urinal and discovered that I was pee-shy. I stood there for a full minute while a man in a Giants cap washed his hands behind me. Nothing came out. I learned the embarrassing truth: even my own body didn't trust me yet.

**Day 4 — The Tourist Family**
A Japanese family came in. The father used the urinal while his tiny son stood next to him, watching with enormous eyes. The father looked down and said something in Japanese. I don't know what it meant. But the boy repeated it, and they both laughed. Love doesn't need a translator.

**Day 5 — The Boy Who Cried at the Sink**
A kid my age came in splashing water on his face. He was wearing a McDonald's uniform and he was crying. He wasn't sobbing. It was the quiet kind of crying that means he'd been holding it in for a long time. I stayed in my stall and did nothing. I wrote that down as a lie: *I did nothing.* You'll learn that from me. I didn't learn it all at once.

**Day 6 — Urinal Geography**
I discovered the unwritten law: never take a urinal directly next to a man if there's an empty one farther away. This is not about courtesy. It's about terror. I mapped the whole room in my notebook with arrows: this is where men stand, this is where they look, this is where they don't look, and this is the exact distance of a stranger's safety.

**Day 7 — The Custodian**
The janitor came in. He was old and Black and he wore coveralls with a name tag that said DELROY. He mopped the same spot for five minutes while singing a gospel song under his breath. When he saw me watching, he said, "Boy, don't you got somewhere to be?" I said I was taking notes. He laughed. "Notes," he said. "I been cleaning this room twenty years. You want notes? The floor knows more than you ever will." And he kept mopping.

**Day 8 — The Flush Rule**
I learned that you should never flush before you're done. It seems stupid. But I saw a man trigger the automatic sensor and then jump back in panic as the toilet swallowed his urine mid-stream. There's a metaphor in there. I'm still working it out.

**Day 9 — The Man Who Talked to Himself**
An elderly man came in wearing a suit that was too warm for September. He talked to himself the whole time. "Okay, okay. Turn left here. No—right. You can do this. You're fine." He was maybe seventy-five. He was arguing with the part of his brain that had started forgetting things. I wrote: *Some wars are private and they happen at the urinal.*

**Day 10 — The Hand Dryer Symphony**
Three men at three sinks. Three hand dryers. One started, then two more, and for a moment the whole room was a terrible machine choir. It was the closest thing to unity I'd seen all week. Strangers, together, in noise.

**Day 11 — The Brick Phone**
A man in a sports coat stood in front of the mirror, holding a brick of a cell phone to his ear. He wasn't talking to anyone. He was just standing there, pretending to listen, looking at himself in the mirror. I saw him switch ears every forty seconds. He was waiting for it to seem real. The phone was a lie. But he needed the lie so badly that I ached for him.

**Day 12 — The Graffiti Poem**
In the stall, on the wall, in careful block letters:

*I am not the body I was given. I am the man I become.*

Underneath, someone else had written: *Then stop eating my lunch.*

I laughed so hard I snorted. The man in the next stall knocked on the wall. "You okay in there?" he asked. That was the first time a stranger knocked.

**Day 13 — The Old Man with the Newspaper**
Old Man Number One. I started counting them that day. He came in with a folded newspaper, went into the handicap stall, and stayed for twenty-three minutes. I heard him read the sports page out loud to himself. When he came out, he washed his hands with water but no soap. He looked at me and said, "Don't tell nobody I forgot." I didn't.

**Day 14 — The Preacher**
A man with a briefcase stood in the middle of the room and announced, "The Lord sees all, brothers. Every hair of your head is numbered." Several men at the urinals zipped up quickly. I don't think he was wrong, but I know he was not helping.

**Day 15 — The Two Guys Who Knew Each Other**
Two men entered separately, but they clearly knew each other. They took two urinals, and the taller one said, "She left me." The shorter one nodded and said, "So you're peeing here because—" "Because I had nowhere else to go." They didn't look at each other. But they stood there, side by side, in silence, until both were done. That was friendship.

**Day 16 — Old Man Number Two**
A tiny man with a cane came in. He had a campaign pin on his lapel that said I LIKE IKE, which was weird because IKE was from the 50s. He stood at the sink, splashing water on his face, and looked at me with the kindest, bluest eyes I have ever seen. "What are you writing, son?" I couldn't answer. He smiled. "Write something true." Then he left. I wrote that down.

**Day 17 — The Perverse Pager**
A teenage boy came in wearing a FUBU shirt and a pager on his belt. He stood at the sink, looked at the pager, and said, "No matter what, page me back. Okay?" Then he star-69'd the wall. I don't think he had anyone to page. The pager was his security blanket, and it would never ring.

**Day 18 — The Slow Zipper**
I watched a man in his fifties take an entire minute to zip his fly. He wasn't shaking. He was savoring it. He stood at the urinal with his eyes closed, and I could see his face slack, his shoulders drop. Whatever he was running from, he was leaving it at the bowl. I wanted that. I still do.

**Day 19 — Old Man Number Three**
A man with a sailor's cap and cloudy eyes came in. He stood at the urinal and said, "Ahoy," to no one. When he was washing his hands, he told the mirror, "The sea doesn't wait." Then he left without drying. I saw later that he'd written his name on the wall: *Sailor Sam, USS Whatever, still here.* I didn't know if he was lost or found. I think he was both.

**Day 20 — The Crossroads of Cologne**
By Day 20, I had learned to identify people by scent. Cheap cologne = someone trying to impress. Soap only = someone in a routine. Alcohol = someone running. Nothing = someone who had given up. I wrote this down in my notebook like a scientist cataloguing species. I didn't realize I was one of them.

# PART TWO: THE REGULARS
## Days 21–40

**Day 21 — Toothbrush Man**
Old Man Number Four came in with a toothbrush in a Ziploc bag. He brushed his teeth at the sink for exactly two minutes. Then he looked at himself in the mirror and said, "Still alive." Pride, I realized, could live in a very small space.

**Day 22 — The Cowboy**
A man in a Stetson and boots walked in, and I mean *walked* like he was crossing open range. He used the urinal, tipped his hat to the mirror, and said, "Thank you, John Wayne." I have no idea who John Wayne was to him. He was probably fifty, and he was still pretending. And it was beautiful.

**Day 23 — The Woman in the Men's Room**
A woman came in. She was maybe sixty, with a scarf tied over gray hair. She looked at me, and I looked at her. She said, "Don't faint, honey. The line for the women's room is down the block, and I gotta pee." She used the stall, washed her hands, and left. Nobody screamed. Nobody fainted. The world did not end. That was the day I understood that gender is a door, not a cage.

**Day 24 — The Father and Son**
A man brought his son, who was maybe six. The boy couldn't reach the urinal, so the father picked him up. The boy laughed. The father laughed. The father said, "Now you're a man." I wrote: *That's it. That's all it takes.*

**Day 25 — The Teen Couple in the Stall**
The handicap stall door clicked shut. I heard two voices, a boy and a girl, whispering. Then I heard the unmistakable sounds of teenage fumbling: a belt buckle, a giggle, a shush, a zipper, another giggle. It was clumsy and human. I sat very still and wrote one word: *Same.*

**Day 26 — The Man Who Cried in the Stall**
Old Man Number Five came in and went into the middle stall. He wasn't sick. He was crying. Not loud. The kind of crying that sounds like the faucet dripping. He stayed for a long time. When he came out, his face was dry but his eyes were red. He didn't look at me. He just washed his hands and said, half to himself, "Good thing water's free." That was the joke he used to cover the pain. I learned that from him.

**Day 27 — The Prophet of the Sink**
A man in paint-stained overalls stood at the sink and said, to nobody, "You don't know what you got 'til you lose it, and you don't know what you lost 'til it's gone." Then he left. I wrote that down in my notebook and underlined it twice.

**Day 28 — The Magician**
Old Man Number Six wore a velvet vest and had a coin in his fingers. He washed his hands, and the coin appeared and disappeared. He caught me staring. "Pick a card," he said. I didn't have a card. He flipped his hand and a card appeared. It was the six of hearts. He said, "That's yours now." I still have it somewhere.

**Day 29 — The Suit with Toilet Paper on His Shoe**
A man in an expensive suit came out of the stall with toilet paper stuck to his shoe. He didn't notice. He washed his hands with terrible posture and left. I thought about telling him. I didn't. For days I regretted it. It's funny what you learn to care about.

**Day 30 — The Farting Competition**
Two teenage boys came in, and as soon as the door closed they started laughing. One said, "Bet you can't do it louder than me." The other said, "Watch this." What followed was a brief and honestly impressive concert. They were gone in thirty seconds, leaving the room with a smell that no amount of pine-scented urinal cake could touch. I wrote: *Boys are animals. Men are just animals with jobs.*

**Day 31 — The Quiet Giant**
Old Man Number Seven was huge. Six foot five, broad hands, a voice that shouldn't have been soft. He was a retired longshoreman, I guessed. He stood at the sink and washed his hands so gently, like they were made of glass. When he dried them, he checked his face in the mirror for a long time. Then he said, "Alright, old man. One more day." That's the whole secret, boys. One more day.

**Day 32 — The Man Who Sang A Cappella**
A man came in with a paper cup in his hand. He took a sip of what I thought was coffee, then started humming. It turned into a full song — something by the Temptations, I think. He sang into the mirror like it was his audience. When he finished, he gave a little bow, drank the rest of his coffee, and left. I applauded silently.

**Day 33 — The Smell of Whiskey**
Another man with the whiskey smell. He was younger than the others. Maybe forty. He stood at the urinal with his elbows on the wall and his forehead resting on his forearms. He didn't zip up for a long time. I wrote: *He is not here. He is somewhere else, and he's trying to drink his way back.*

**Day 34 — The Tourist Map**
Old Man Number Eight came in carrying a map of the city. He stood in front of the mirror, unfolded the map, and turned it every direction, trying to figure out where he was. I looked over his shoulder. We were at the map's center, listed in fine print. I pointed. He laughed. "Well, I'll be," he said. "You can't get lost in a bathroom. That's the trick."

**Day 35 — The Man Who Brought a Book**
A man came in, sat in the middle stall, and read for a full hour. I know because I timed him. When he came out, he was holding a paperback with a bookmark halfway through. He didn't wash his hands. He just left. I wanted to be that unbothered.

**Day 36 — The Poet's Stall**
On the wall of the dumpiest stall, someone had written a haiku:

*Urinal cake sits / like a green and sunny moon / don't eat it, my friend.*

Underneath, in different handwriting: *Too late.*

I laughed so hard the man at the sink laughed with me even though he didn't know why.
